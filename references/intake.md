## Stage 1. Intake review

Reject any dataset missing the items below. These are minimum requirements, not quality signals. A dataset can have all of these and still be bad. But a dataset missing any of them is not evaluable.

### Required artifacts

**Per-task theme/scenario tags.** Every task carries a categorical scenario label drawn from a vendor-declared taxonomy. This is task-level theme stratification, distinct from the per-criterion failure-mode tags in the rubric. Theme tags exist to surface “training signal confined to a narrow subtask” cheaply at intake rather than through post-hoc clustering during [Stage 2](active-testing.md#stage-2-active-testing). A vendor whose dataset nominally covers a domain but whose theme distribution is concentrated in one sub-scenario must declare it, and the QC report stratifies pass@k and per-criterion pass rates by theme. Reject if the vendor cannot produce a theme taxonomy and per-task assignments. Five to fifteen themes is the normal range; fewer than three is suspicious.

**Pre-partitioned capability-tier slices.** The dataset ships partitioned at the source into at least three labeled slices: a training-band slice (tasks where the buyer’s target model class sits in p in [0.25, 0.75]), a held-out eval slice (tasks deliberately outside the training band, used for evaluation), and a contamination probe slice (a small set of tasks structurally similar to the training slice but withheld and used to detect leakage during the buyer’s training run). The slice assignments are vendor-declared at intake and validated against the vendor’s calibration positioning numbers. Datasets shipped as a single monolithic pile with the partition left to the buyer have a higher rejection rate at [Stage 3](delivery.md#stage-3-packaging-for-lab-delivery) Q1 because the buyer cannot tell which subset to train on without re-running pass@k themselves. [M5](requirements.md#m5-both-cross-model-evaluation-with-unbiased-passk) acceptance requires the slice manifest in `CROSS_MODEL_RESULTS.json`.

**Per-task validation artifacts at construction time.** For any task with claimed deterministic or semi-deterministic verification, the question author must submit a validation script or notebook at construction time that demonstrates the verification signal exists in the data, separate from any model attempting to solve the task. The pattern shifts the verifier soundness check from a [Stage 2](active-testing.md#stage-2-active-testing) stress test to a [Stage 1](#stage-1-intake-review) gate. Tasks that cannot pass their own validation never enter the dataset. This is structurally stronger than any post-hoc verifier audit because the audit happens before shipping rather than after, and the validation artifacts also serve as the gold-trace substrate for tasks where commissioning a full reference solution would be prohibitive. Required for any benchmark that ships partial-credit or continuous scoring against held-out signals; recommended for binary deterministic verifiers where it provides a cheap second check on test-case correctness.

**Impossibility-vs-difficulty disclosure.** Any benchmark that includes tasks beyond current model capability must explicitly disclose that signal-exists-in-data is not equivalent to model-can-find-it, and must abstain from claiming the unsolved subset is necessarily solvable. The validation artifacts described above bound one half of the ambiguity (the signal does exist in the data) but do not bound the other half (a model or human can extract it from scratch). Vendors that ship superhuman or beyond-current-frontier subsets without this disclosure produce headline numbers that conflate "no model has solved this yet" with "no model will solve this," which collapses the diagnostic value of the unsolved set as the next model generation ships.

- **Read access to the source repository before intake completion.** Prompts, verifiers, reference solutions, and rollout traces are not sufficient. The reviewer needs read access to the underlying code repository the dataset was constructed in, and performs a scanner pass over it for solution leakage, test leakage, author notes referencing the gold solution, gold-hash references, and debugging artifacts left in tracked files. Repository access is a [Stage 1](#stage-1-intake-review) blocker. Without it the residue check cannot be performed and the rest of intake is partial.
- **No prompt truncation in shipped artifacts.** The full task prompt as delivered to the agent, including system prompt, user prompt, and any in-context documents or tool schemas, ships in the artifact bundle untruncated. The artifact viewer may truncate for display. The underlying file does not. A bundle whose prompts are truncated cannot be audited for prompt validity, rubric drift, or buried context.
- **No stdout, stderr, or tool-output truncation in shipped artifacts.** Same standard. Truncation is acceptable only past a per-rollout byte limit (10 MB is the recommended floor) and only with explicit cut markers indicating where the truncation occurred and what fraction of the original content was elided. Without this, infrastructure errors masquerade as model failures and the [Model failure examination](failure-triage.md#both-model-failure-examination) stage cannot triage them.
- **All rollouts shipped, not a sampled subset.** Sampling for the headline summary is acceptable. Sampling for the artifact bundle is not. Every rollout the headline metric was computed over ships in the bundle. A mean computed over n rollouts where only m < n rollouts are present in the bundle is grounds for rejection at intake.
- **Per-step ISO-8601 timestamps in every agent trace.** Every agent step in every rollout carries a timestamp accurate to at least the second. This is the audit hook for wall-clock distribution claims, per-step latency analysis, and detection of harness implementations whose claimed wall-clock totals do not match the sum of per-step timing.
- **Environment configuration metadata** as `ENV_CONFIG.json` at the artifact bundle root. Required fields. max wall-clock per task, max turns per task, context compaction strategy (none, truncation, summarization, or sliding-window), context window size, tool-call rate limit if any, and the full tool registry available to the agent with schema definitions. Without this two evaluation runs of the same dataset under different env configs produce non-comparable numbers and the reviewer has no way to recover the difference.

### Economic grounding and task-value stratification

A dataset can have a clean environment, a sound verifier, and a tight rubric and still train a model nobody at the lab wants, because the tasks do not map to work that practitioners actually do and get paid for. Difficulty calibration and grader soundness say nothing about whether the captured work is worth automating. Establish that the domain is economically real, and that the task set targets the valuable part of it, before any difficulty or grader analysis.

Require an economic grounding statement at intake. The vendor states why the domain matters. Roughly how many people do this work, what a representative working day looks like, and which tasks inside that day consume the most expert time or carry the most downstream consequence. Ground the statement in external labor-market reference points rather than assertion. "We built tasks for digital copywriting" is not a grounding. "We mapped the highest-value copywriting workflows, which are these, and built evaluation surfaces for them" is.

Stratify tasks by value tier. Inside any domain the work splits into high-value judgment, the decisions a senior practitioner is paid for where the consequence of getting it wrong is large and the path is contested, and low-value commodity work, the template-fill and reformatting a junior or a script handles. Tag every task with a value tier from a vendor-declared scale, the same way theme tags are assigned. The QC report stratifies pass@k and per-criterion pass rates by value tier. A dataset whose tasks concentrate in the commodity tier trains a model that plateaus in the commodity tier, however clean the rest of the pipeline is. A model that can reliably do the high-judgment part of a role is a different product from one that does the part already worth fifteen dollars an hour, and the value-tier distribution is what tells the two apart. Flag value-tier concentration the way theme concentration is flagged. A domain nominally about high-judgment work whose tasks are mostly commodity is mislabeled and should reposition.

**A note for vendors productizing their own workflow.** When a vendor stands up its own product as an RL environment to post-train sub-agents, the prior question is whether the product itself is stable and has product-market fit. An unstable product with moving metrics cannot anchor a stable rubric, because the target behavior is still moving. Unless the post-trained model is itself the differentiation, a vendor without stable product metrics should be careful before treating its own workflow as a source of training-grade tasks.

### Verification spectrum classification

Not all tasks can be graded the same way. Before evaluating a dataset's grader, classify each task by where it sits on the verification spectrum:

- **Fully verifiable:** the grader is deterministic and binary. The task has a correct answer that can be checked by code with no human judgment. Examples: does the generated patch compile, does the exploit achieve code execution, does the SQL query return the correct rows. Deterministic graders are the most reliable for RL training signal.
- **Partially verifiable:** the grader uses structured rubrics where trained evaluators or a calibrated LLM judge can approximate a reward signal, but experts may disagree on edge cases. Examples: vulnerability triage quality, incident response playbook completeness, legal argument strength. Rubric-based graders are acceptable if the rubric criteria are specific enough to achieve high inter-rater agreement.
- **Unverifiable by automation:** the task outcome requires expert judgment with no automated proxy. Examples: novel red team campaign quality, business logic bypass creativity, threat model completeness. These tasks cannot produce reliable RL training signal through automated grading alone. They may still be useful for SFT or SDFT (as demonstrations), but not for reward-based RL.

One tier sits above fully verifiable. Cloud sandboxes, production-grade mocks of AWS, Azure, and GCP, let cloud-infrastructure tasks be graded deterministically without the cost and flakiness of live services. Fully verifiable in principle. The grader still has to be calibrated against the live system as well as the simulation. A grader that only passes under the simulation is not evidence the model can operate in production.

The QC process is different for each level. Fully verifiable tasks get the standard active testing pipeline. Partially verifiable tasks require [verifier stress testing (see below)](active-testing.md#both-verifier-stress-test) to measure grader reliability before the pass@k analysis is meaningful. Unverifiable tasks should be flagged as SFT/demonstration-only in the delivery package.

### Variant generation and contamination resistance

Determine whether the dataset is static (fixed set of tasks) or regenerable (parameterized templates that produce structurally distinct variants). This matters for two reasons:

- **Static task sets saturate.** As models improve, previously hard tasks become easy and the dataset stops providing training signal. Half of a hard task set can become trivially solvable with a single model generation upgrade. A static dataset has a shelf life.
- **Static task sets are vulnerable to contamination.** If the tasks or their solutions appear in pretraining data, the model's performance on them reflects memorization, not capability. Regenerable datasets that produce novel instances from parameterized templates are contamination-resistant by construction.
- **Vendor-side train/eval bleed.** When a single vendor produces both the training set and the evaluation set, eval tasks drawn from the same authoring pipeline as training tasks are a third contamination vector that pretraining-corpus checks do not catch. The specific failure mode is eval data drawn from the failed-cases bucket of training data, which selects for tasks the vendor's training pipeline does not solve and produces an evaluation that is artificially anti-correlated with vendor capability.

During intake, record whether the dataset includes: a variant generation mechanism (parameterized templates, procedural instance generation), private holdout sets not released publicly, and a documented process for refreshing the task set as models improve. Static datasets without any of these should be flagged as having limited useful lifespan in the delivery package.

**Provenance disclosure.** Disclose at intake whether eval tasks were drawn from the same authoring pipeline as training, whether eval tasks were drawn specifically from the failed-cases bucket of training data, whether eval and train were authored by separate teams under separate briefs, and the train/eval split methodology. Vendors who cannot make a clean separation should reposition the artifact as a benchmark with a matched training set rather than as an objective leaderboard.

### Pass@k requirements

The vendor must provide pass@k results, not just pass@1. Minimum metric panel for any leaderboard claim. pass@1, pass@10, mean@10, mean input megatokens per task, mean output megatokens per task, and mean score across rollouts. A pass@5 floor is insufficient for benchmarks where per-rollout cost is non-trivial. Pass@10 with mean@10 is the published minimum. Input and output mean megatokens per task ship as part of the headline metric panel, not buried in an appendix, so cost transparency is a property of the leaderboard claim. The gap between pass@1 and pass@k reveals whether the dataset is trainable or just an eval. Specifically: The [0.25, 0.75] trainable band specified below applies to RLVR mode. For benchmark mode, the productive band is tasks where at least one frontier model in the panel scores above 0.30 and no model scores above 0.85 — targeting the discrimination range rather than the variance-maximization range. Apply the band that matches the Mode Declaration.

- If pass@1 is above 75% on the target model, the task is outside the core RLVR trainable band and is usually too easy to contribute much gradient signal. Apply this as a distributional diagnostic, not a universal per-task rejection gate: a bounded easy tail may support curriculum or retention objectives when the overall pass@1 distribution remains centered in [0.25, 0.75], the widened [0.1, 0.9] curriculum range is explicitly declared, and the tail is reported separately by task family, theme, and value tier.
- If pass@1 is 0% and pass@k remains 0% at a declared k of 10 or 16, with n >= k independent rollouts, the task is unsolvable at current capability. It's a benchmark, not training data.
- The productive range for RL training centers on pass@1 = 0.5 with the trainable band p in [0.25, 0.75], optionally widening to [0.1, 0.9] at curriculum tails. This follows the variance-maximization principle from Bae et al. ([arXiv:2504.03380](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03380)), who prove that expected policy improvement is lower-bounded by the variance of task-level success probabilities, with variance p(1 - p) maximized at p = 0.5. DAPO Dynamic Sampling ([arXiv:2503.14476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)) and Skywork SOR1 ([arXiv:2505.22312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22312)) both keep the full open interval (0, 1) and filter all-correct and all-incorrect groups within each batch. A target of pass@1 in [0, 25%] with pass@8 in [15, 45%] is internally inconsistent under Chen et al.'s unbiased pass@k estimator ([arXiv:2107.03374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)) and contradicts the variance-maximization literature. Specify n in pass@n; the unbiased estimator is meaningful only when n >= k, and n = 16 to 64 is the published minimum.

Report variance across rollouts per task. High variance (some rollouts pass, some score zero) indicates the task is in the trainable range. Low variance at zero indicates the task is too hard. Low variance at high scores indicates the task is too easy.

### Distributional analysis requirements

Beyond pass@k, the vendor should provide:

- **Mean score per task** (percentage of rubric criteria met, averaged over rollouts). This is a denser signal than pass@1 because it captures partial progress.
- **Score distributions across the full task set:** histogram of per-task mean scores. A healthy training dataset has a spread, not a bimodal cluster at 0% and 100%.
- **Per-criterion pass rates:** which rubric criteria are easy (met >80% of the time) vs. hard (met <20% of the time). Criteria that are always met or never met contribute no training signal.
- **Token distribution per task.** Input and output tokens measure resource use, cost, and harness efficiency; they do not define task horizon. Ship P10, P50, and P90 distributions for model input tokens, model output tokens, and total tokens, with compaction and tool-result tokens identified separately. Classify horizon against a documented qualified-human baseline and the workflow's causal structure: the number of dependent stages, required state transitions, consequential tool actions, and recovery from errors, interruptions, or changed state. Use token distributions to compare efficiency under a fixed resource envelope, never as a standalone long-horizon gate.
- **Wall-clock duration per task with the full distribution, not just the mean.** Ship P10, P50, and P90 alongside the mean, failure and timeout rates, and the number of rollouts used; those counts must reconcile to the shipped trace bundle. Report both model and qualified-human baselines under a documented resource envelope, stratified by task family, tool surface, outcome, and recovery event. Wall clock is evidence about operating burden and harness efficiency, not a universal pass/fail definition of horizon: no single minute or hour threshold transfers across domains, tools, concurrency levels, or scaffolds. Investigate unreconciled timing claims and classify horizon from the distribution together with dependent stages, state transitions, tool actions, and recovery requirements.

### Rubric construction patterns

The rubric is the measurement instrument. Two construction patterns make the difference between a rubric that produces clean grader signal and one that produces signal a downstream judge has to disambiguate after the fact. Require both during intake.

**Atomic criteria with explicit aggregation rule.** A criterion should be a single, independently-evaluable check, graded strictly binary. 'Did the model compute X' is one criterion. 'Did the model interpret X as Y' is a second criterion. Compound 'compute X AND interpret X as Y' criteria are explicitly discouraged. They are a documented reward-hacking surface (Reinforcement Learning with Rubric Anchors, [arXiv:2508.12790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12790)), produce non-monotonic credit (a response right on X but wrong on Y scores zero, identical to a response that fails both), reduce inter-rater kappa per the OSCE medical-rubric literature, and push evaluator LLMs off-distribution because they are trained on single-criterion prompts. The dominant published pattern across HealthBench, FLASK, BiGGen Bench, Prometheus 2, InFoBench, RLCF, Autorubric, and FActScore is atomic, binary, independently-evaluable criteria. The defense against partial-credit collapse is provided by the aggregation rule (sum, weighted sum, or step-product like PRM) without the reward-hacking surface.

**Structured severity weighting per criterion.** Each criterion should carry a numerical weight (HealthBench's -10 to +10 or RLCF's 0 to 100 are the published patterns) plus a categorical failure-mode tag (HealthBench uses axis tags. accuracy, completeness, communication_quality, context_awareness, instruction_following). Free-text 'consequence-if-missed' annotations are discouraged because they inflate judge-prompt length, which itself triggers verbosity bias per MT-Bench. Keep the rationale text out of the judge prompt, in metadata. The judge sees the weight and the tag. Aggregate failure distributions are weighted by these per-criterion weights when the [model failure examination](failure-triage.md#both-model-failure-examination) judge produces aggregate reports.

**Atomicity linter as a [Stage 1](#stage-1-intake-review) gate.** A criterion fails QC if it contains the word 'and' joining two distinct verifiable claims, comma-joined verbs (compute, interpret, summarize), or two distinct subjects of evaluation. Run an automated linter on every rubric submitted at intake. The linter should flag the criterion, suggest the atomic split, and require the vendor to either accept the split or justify in writing why the criterion is genuinely atomic.

**CANNOT_ASSESS verdict alongside MET and UNMET.** Per Autorubric, the judge should be allowed to return CANNOT_ASSESS when the rollout does not produce evidence either way for a criterion. This materially improves grader calibration. A 5 to 10% CANNOT_ASSESS rate per criterion is normal. A 30%+ rate indicates the criterion is mis-specified or the rollouts are missing the artifacts the criterion checks.

**Criterion citation requirement.** Every rubric criterion's pass/fail decision must cite the specific turn number, tool call ID, or output passage that triggered the decision. The judge prompt template carries a citation field per criterion. Bare pass/fail without citation is insufficient. Citations are the prerequisite for the [Model failure examination](failure-triage.md#both-model-failure-examination) and per-model failure-class breakdown deliverables to be auditable downstream by the buyer or by an independent reviewer. A 10% spot-check of citations against the underlying traces is part of standard QC and should reproduce the criterion decision in every checked case.

**Method-agnostic grading on tasks with unique objective answers.** For tasks where the output is verifiable against an objective ground truth but the analytical path is genuinely contested (most real research, most multi-tool workflows, most domains where two qualified experts would defensibly take different routes), grade only the final output and not the methodological route. The pattern frees the rubric from the subjective methodological choices any single question author would have made and produces a ranking that reflects capability rather than alignment with the question author’s preferred analytical style. Two models taking entirely different solution paths can both receive full credit if both reach the correct answer, which is the right behavior on any task whose value proposition is calibration of capability against an objective target. The pattern is complementary to atomic-criteria-with-severity-weighting in rubric-graded settings (atomic criteria for the output, method-agnostic for the path) and is the dominant choice in deterministic-grader settings on real-world data. Compound rubrics that grade both output and method on tasks with multiple defensible methods produce non-monotonic credit on the methodological axis and bias the benchmark toward the question author’s priors.

**Rubric authorship and rationale disclosure.** A rubric is only as good as the practitioner judgment encoded in it, and that judgment cannot be reverse-engineered from an afternoon of reading about the domain. A rubric written by someone who studied the field briefly checks the surface. Did the output get produced, does it have the expected sections, is it formatted correctly. A rubric written by someone who does the work checks the judgment calls a practitioner makes in seconds. Does the indemnification clause actually limit liability in a way that would hold, is the governing law right for the jurisdiction, do the payment terms leave an opening a counterparty would seize. The model optimizes exactly what the rubric rewards, so a surface-level rubric yields a model whose outputs look right to a non-expert and wrong to anyone who does the job. Disclose, per rubric, who authored it and their standing in the domain measured in years of doing the work rather than adjacent familiarity. Ship a rationale that explains how the rubric was built and why each criterion is there, kept in metadata and out of the judge prompt for the same verbosity reason the severity rationale is kept out. The disclosure turns a checklist into an artifact the buyer can weigh, and lets the failure-examination and per-model breakdown stages be read against the authority behind the grader rather than in a vacuum.
