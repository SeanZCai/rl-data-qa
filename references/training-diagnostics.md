### [RL-only] Catastrophic forgetting check

Run the post-trained model on a standard general-capability eval suite (MMLU, GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, IFEval, AlpacaEval) and report per-skill degradation rather than aggregate. Acceptable degradation per skill is less than 1 standard deviation of the benchmark's natural variance, or less than 3% absolute, whichever is tighter. A blanket threshold of less than 2% degradation on aggregate is not anchored in any published threshold from Tulu 3, Llama 3, or Anthropic. Tulu 3 ([arXiv:2411.15124](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124)) reports per-skill ablations rather than aggregate budgets. Aggregate metrics under 2% can mask severe localized regressions. Qi et al. and Zhan et al. ([arXiv:2311.05553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05553)) demonstrate that small benign fine-tunes can remove RLHF safety guardrails while leaving aggregate capability untouched. Run a decoupled safety audit on HarmBench, WildGuardTest, XSTest, and WildJailbreak independently of aggregate quality. For multi-domain datasets, run the forgetting check after each domain's training data is applied sequentially. A well-constructed dataset allows sequential training without domains interfering with each other. RL-based post-training tends to forget less than SFT (Cameron Wolfe summary. SFT-driven continual post-training shows average forgetting around -10.4%; on-policy RL around -2.3%); naive sequential SFT for multi-domain is not recommended.

For multi-domain datasets, run the forgetting check after each domain's training data is applied sequentially. A well-constructed dataset allows sequential training without domains interfering with each other.

### [Both] Paired-model regression check

Run the same eval against the previous-generation model from the same lab family and the current-generation model. Catastrophic forgetting measures what a new model loses against general capability benchmarks. The paired-model regression check measures whether the dataset's rubric still discriminates across model versions. A rubric that returns the same headline score for last-generation and current-generation models is not a measurement instrument, it is a floor. The eval has saturated against the rubric's coarseness rather than against the underlying capability.

**Acceptable signal.** The rubric shows a meaningful uplift from N-1 to N, the per-criterion failure rates shift, and the failure-mode distribution from the [model failure examination](failure-triage.md#both-model-failure-examination) pass changes shape. If the dataset shows neither uplift nor distributional shift across model generations, retire it or rewrite the rubric.

For datasets that intentionally target only frontier capability, extend the comparison to three strata. The current frontier model, one generation back, and a small open model. The expected pattern is monotonic. A non-monotonic pattern across the three strata indicates either a verifier defect, a scoring artifact, or a coarse rubric, and the dataset cannot be packaged as a frontier capability eval until the source of the non-monotonicity is identified.

### [RL-only — SFT/SDFT specifically] Log-probability shift analysis

For datasets structured as (query, demonstration) pairs, measure the log-probability shift per example:

```
shift = log p(demonstration | query, demonstration_prefix) - log p(demonstration | query)
```

This measures how much the demonstration changes the model's distribution over responses. Three outcomes:

- **Negligible shift (near zero):** the demonstration is not teaching the model anything it didn't already know. The example is noise.
- **Large positive shift:** the demonstration is informative. The model's distribution moves toward the demonstrated behavior.
- **Large negative shift:** the demonstration contradicts the model's existing distribution. This could mean the demonstration is wrong, or the model has a strong prior that conflicts. Inspect manually.

Report the distribution of shifts across the dataset. A healthy dataset has mostly positive shifts with a long right tail. A dataset that's mostly noise (near-zero shifts) is not worth training on regardless of what the vendor claims.

### [RL-only] Frontier shape analysis

This diagnostic uses the Pareto frontier across cost, latency, and performance to detect faulty RL environments and poorly designed task sets. The qualitative framework synthesizes published Pareto-curve work (HAL [arXiv:2510.11977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11977) is the closest published analog plotting accuracy-vs-cost across nine benchmarks and nine models; Snell et al. [arXiv:2408.03314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314); Wu et al. ICLR 2025 [arXiv:2408.00724](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00724); TTSPM [arXiv:2505.20522](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20522) for parametric saturation; Brown et al. [arXiv:2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787) and Schaeffer et al. [arXiv:2502.17578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17578) for frontier smoothness; Gao et al. [arXiv:2210.10760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760) and Rafailov et al. [arXiv:2406.02900](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02900) for the reward-hacking signature). The qualitative signatures (reward-hackable, coarse-rubric, misaligned-reward, contaminated-task) are defensible by analogy. The specific numeric thresholds (phase divergence cutoffs at 0.05 and 0.15, saturation speed normal range 2x to 8x) are heuristic calibration values that require validation against labeled cases. Use the qualitative framework as a diagnostic; treat the specific cutoffs as heuristics, not validated acceptance thresholds.

#### Procedure

- For each task set under evaluation, run two post-training configurations on the GLM 4.6 32B instance (or equivalent small model already used for stage 2 testing): (a) two-phase training, where phase 1 maximizes capability with no latency penalty and phase 2 adds a latency penalty to compress the learned capability into efficient behavior, and (b) joint training with a single blended reward combining task performance and latency cost.
- For each configuration, sample at 4-6 points along the latency budget: 1x, 2x, 4x, 8x, 16x the baseline token count. At each point, measure task performance using the vendor's own grader. This produces the raw frontier: a set of (latency, performance) pairs for each configuration.
- Compute the three diagnostic metrics described below for each task set. Flag any task set where two or more metrics fall outside the normal range. A single anomaly may be noise; correlated anomalies across frontier metrics are diagnostic.
- For flagged task sets, prescribe remediation based on the specific pathology identified ([see diagnostic signatures below](#diagnostic-signatures-and-remediation)).

#### Diagnostic metrics

Compute these three values for each task set's frontier:

**Frontier smoothness.** Fit a monotone spline to the performance-vs-latency curve and measure residuals. High residuals (large deviations from a smooth curve) indicate discrete jumps in the frontier, which means the grader's rubric has insufficient resolution. A smooth frontier means the reward signal has enough granularity to guide incremental improvements. A stepped frontier means the grader can only distinguish a few discrete performance levels (pass/fail with no partial credit), so there's no gradient between 'miss entirely' and 'nail it.' When you can't linearize the reward because the rubric has no continuous structure, the frontier itself shows you.

**Saturation speed.** The latency multiplier at which performance reaches 90% of its maximum value on the frontier. Abnormally low saturation speed (below 2x baseline) means the model finds a high-scoring strategy early with minimal additional reasoning, which indicates either: the task is too easy (already diagnosed by pass@k, but this confirms it from a different angle), the task is contaminated and the model is pattern-matching from pretraining, or the reward is hackable and the model found an exploit that doesn't require deep reasoning. Abnormally high saturation speed (above 16x baseline) means additional compute keeps buying performance improvements far beyond what's normal, which may indicate the task is beyond the model's capability ceiling or the grader has a scaling artifact. Normal range: 2x-8x for well-designed tasks in the productive difficulty range.

**Phase divergence.** The area between the two-phase frontier curve and the joint-trained frontier curve, normalized by the area under the two-phase curve. This is the most diagnostic metric. Training capability and latency objectives simultaneously produces local optima because the objectives interfere with each other. In a well-designed task set, the two-phase approach (build capability first, then compress) should outperform joint training, but only modestly, because the reward signal is aligned with genuine capability. A large phase divergence (above 0.15 normalized area) means the reward signal is fighting the capability signal: the model can develop deep understanding in phase 1 but can't compress it in phase 2 because the reward is measuring something other than the actual task capability. This indicates reward misalignment, where the grader is measuring surface features, format compliance, or proxy metrics rather than the intended skill. A small phase divergence (below 0.05) means the reward is well-aligned and the two training approaches converge on similar solutions.

#### Discrete-benchmark variant. Per-problem solve consistency.

The frontier-shape diagnostics above require continuous scoring and multiple latency budgets. For binary-pass benchmarks where these conditions do not apply, the discrete analog is per-problem solve consistency analysis. Run each problem n times (n = 5 to 16, depending on compute budget) on each evaluated model, and report the per-problem solve count distribution (0/n, 1/n, ... n/n) as a histogram. Compute two summary statistics. The reliable-solve fraction is the share of problems the model solves at >= 4/5 (or equivalent threshold for higher n) and represents reproducible task success. The brittle-win fraction is the share of problems the model solves at 1/5 or 2/5 (or equivalent low end) and represents stochastic wins the model cannot reproduce under the same observable task and harness conditions. The reliable-to-brittle ratio is the one-number diagnostic for whether headline accuracy reflects repeatable capability or high-variance success. A model with 80% headline accuracy that splits 70% reliable / 5% brittle is a different artifact from one with 80% accuracy that splits 40% reliable / 35% brittle, and aggregate accuracy alone obscures the difference. The diagnostic is especially load-bearing on benchmarks that include subsets beyond current capability, where the brittle-win share rises sharply on the hard subset relative to the easy subset and exposes the boundary at which repeatable capability gives way to sporadic success. This metric does not replace the continuous frontier-shape analysis where it applies, but it is the right substitute on discrete-pass benchmarks and on small-N task sets where multiple latency budgets are not feasible.

#### Diagnostic signatures and remediation

Each frontier pathology maps to a specific defect in the task set or its grader:

**Reward-hackable tasks (low saturation speed + low phase divergence).** The frontier shows a near-vertical rise in the low-latency region followed by a hard plateau. The model finds an exploit early, saturates, and additional reasoning depth buys nothing. The frontier collapses to a point rather than tracing a curve. Remediation: the reward signal needs redesign, not more data. Inspect what the high-scoring low-latency solutions are actually doing. They're likely exploiting a rubric gap or grader artifact.

**Coarse rubric (high frontier residuals, stepped curve).** The frontier shows discrete performance levels with dead zones between them. The grader can only score at a few levels so the model has no gradient to follow between them. Remediation: add partial-credit tiers to the rubric. Each rubric criterion should target a specific sub-step, and the grader should score criteria independently so that incremental improvements register in the reward signal.

**Misaligned reward (high phase divergence).** The two-phase frontier significantly outperforms the joint frontier. Phase 1 develops genuine capability but phase 2 can't compress it because the reward points somewhere else. Remediation: audit what the grader is actually measuring versus what the task description claims. Common culprits: grader checks format compliance rather than correctness, grader rewards verbose outputs because length correlates with rubric criterion count, or grader measures a proxy metric that diverges from actual task completion at high performance levels.

**Contaminated or memorizable tasks (low saturation speed + near-ceiling performance at 1x).** The frontier shows an outlier in the upper-left corner: high performance at minimal compute. The model achieves near-ceiling performance without extended reasoning, which no other task set in the same domain achieves. This is the hillclimbness problem from a different angle: if the model doesn't need additional compute to solve the task, the Pareto curve confirms there's nothing to trade off. Remediation: apply variant generation (see intake review section on contamination resistance). If the vendor provides parameterized templates, regenerate the task instances and re-run. If performance drops significantly with novel instances, the original results reflected memorization.

#### Cost and infrastructure

Frontier shape analysis uses short training runs, not full post-training. Phase 1 runs approximately 1000 steps and phase 2 approximately 500 steps. You're running on the 32B model, not a frontier-scale model. A single H100 node running 6 frontier points across 2 configurations for one task set finishes in hours, not days. The infrastructure is the same GLM 4.6 32B instance already stood up for the other stage 2 tests (reward hacking probes, catastrophic forgetting check, log-probability shift analysis), so the marginal cost is the additional training runs, not new infrastructure. This is significantly cheaper than shipping a bad dataset to a lab, having them train on it, and discovering the reward hacking post-deployment.

The Pareto frontier is not just a tool for optimizing a model's inference profile. It's a quality assurance instrument for the training data itself. The frontier's shape encodes information about the reward signal's structure, and that structure is exactly what you're auditing when you QC a post-training dataset.
